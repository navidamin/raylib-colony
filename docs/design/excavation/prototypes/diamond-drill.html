A diamond bit does not chip rock loose, it grinds it away, tooth by tooth, and only inside a band of weight it can actually deliver. Both rigs below take the same two commands and answer differently: the compact runs out of weight before basalt does, and the heavy runs out of steadiness before depth does. Drive them and watch which one is wrong for the ground.

Why two rigs and not one upgrade. A diamond bit cuts only inside a band of weight: too little and it polishes — the diamond glazes over, the rock heats and nothing advances; too much and it overloads, jamming the string and breaking what it lifts. The band moves up with hardness, so the compact's whole range sits neatly inside soft ground and misses basalt's band entirely, while the heavy covers basalt and overshoots regolith. Neither is a strict upgrade — the machine table's rule (excavation_constants.h) holds here too.

What depth costs the heavy. Rotation is what a long string turns into wobble, and wobble is what kills a bit. The mid-body stabiliser buys back a fixed amount of it; past that the only remedy is to ease the rotation as the hole deepens, which is slower, which is the decision.

The open question this raises. Access in excavation-design.md is a shaft you pay for and sink blind — and the deepest ground is the least surveyed. But run with a core barrel in the string, this rig comes back up holding the column it cut. If the shaft is the survey, then driving it gently buys knowledge and driving it hard buys only the hole, and siting a deep shaft stops being a pure gamble. The core strip beside each stratigraphy column is that proposal, drawn.
